You text your friend a long, funny message. Three paragraphs. Two emojis. And she replies: "ok." With a period. And just like that... your whole evening is gone. What did I do? Is she mad at me?

If your brain does this, you're not broken. You're running very old software. So why does a two-letter text feel like a threat? And why are you almost always wrong about it?

Here's the first problem. A text has no face. No voice. No smile. In person, your brain reads tone from a hundred tiny signals. In a message, almost all of them are missing. In a well-known study, psychologists Justin Kruger and Nicholas Epley found that people were confident their emails would be read the way they meant them. But readers trying to tell sarcastic messages from serious ones did barely better than a coin flip. So when the signals go missing... your brain doesn't leave a blank. It fills it in. And a worried brain fills it in with the worst version.

Even the period isn't neutral. In one small study, one-word text replies that ended with a period were rated as less sincere than the same word without one. So your friend typed two letters and a dot. And your brain wrote a whole story.

Now here's where it gets personal. Psychologists Geraldine Downey and Scott Feldman described something called rejection sensitivity. Some of us anxiously expect rejection. And when you're expecting it, you're more likely to see it in moments that are simply... unclear. A short reply. A late reply. A "seen" with no answer. The Shadow doesn't need evidence. It just needs a gap.

And here's the twist. In 2018, researchers led by Erica Boothby had strangers talk to each other, then asked two questions. How much did you like your partner? And how much do you think they liked you? Again and again, people underestimated how much they were liked. The researchers called it the liking gap. We walk away from conversations thinking we said something weird... while the other person is thinking, "That was nice."

Which brings us back to Juno. Because Juno wasn't mad. Juno was on a crowded bus, typing with one thumb. And right now, Juno is staring at her phone, thinking... "Was 'ok.' too short? Is Milo mad at me?"

So next time your brain says, "They're mad at you," try this. Notice the move. That's called mind reading, and your brain is guessing, not knowing. Then come up with three other reasons for the reply. They're busy. They're tired. They just text like that. And if it still bothers you... ask. One honest "Hey, are we good?" can end a whole night of guessing.

Most of the time, the answer is: "Of course. Why?"
